A commercial fit out that runs over budget rarely fails because of one big mistake. It's usually a combination of incomplete surveys, late design changes, and vague pricing that chips away at the numbers until the final invoice bears little resemblance to the original quotation.

The good news is that most overruns are preventable — once you know where they come from and how to control them. This guide covers the common causes of budget overruns, how to set a realistic budget before work begins, and the practical steps that keep costs on track throughout delivery.

## Why Commercial Fit Out Projects Go Over Budget

Managing commercial fit out projects on a fixed budget comes down to three things: strict scope control, detailed pre-construction planning, and a sensible contingency reserve. When any of these slip, costs follow. The frustrating part? Most overruns are entirely avoidable once you know where they come from.

### Incomplete Pre-Construction Surveys

Hidden problems discovered mid-project are expensive. Asbestos behind ceiling tiles, outdated wiring that can't handle modern loads, or structural issues that weren't visible during the initial walkthrough — all of these force unplanned spending.

A proper site survey before pricing catches these risks early. Your contractor can then include them in the quotation rather than presenting them as variations later. The survey cost is typically a fraction of what you'd spend fixing surprises once trades are already on site.

### Late Design Changes and Scope Creep

Scope creep is the gradual expansion of what a project includes after work has started. A different floor finish here, an extra glass partition there — each change seems small, but they accumulate quickly.

Here's the thing: changes made during design cost far less than changes made during construction. Locking your specification before signing contracts is one of the simplest ways to protect your budget. Once the plasterers have finished and the electricians have first-fixed, moving a wall becomes a very different conversation.

### Underestimated Contingency

Contingency is the portion of your budget set aside for genuine unknowns. It's not a slush fund or a nice-to-have — it's a planned line item for the things you can't predict.

Too often, contingency is either forgotten or set unrealistically low. Without adequate allowance, any surprise forces difficult decisions about cutting scope or finding additional funds at short notice.

### Poor Contractor Coordination

When multiple subcontractors work to different schedules, delays and rework become almost inevitable. One trade waiting for another creates downtime. Miscommunication leads to work being done twice.

Contractors with in-house teams working to a single programme avoid much of this friction. Everyone reports to the same project manager, follows the same schedule, and takes responsibility for the same outcome.

### Unclear Specifications and Vague Estimates

Open-book pricing and provisional sums might seem flexible, but they leave you exposed. If the specification isn't detailed, the final cost won't be either.

- **Provisional sums:** Allowances for work that hasn't been fully specified — often exceeded once details are confirmed
- **Daywork rates:** Hourly charges for work outside the original scope — can accumulate quickly without clear controls
- **Open-book contracts:** Transparency on costs, but no ceiling on what you'll ultimately pay

Fixed-price quotations based on clear specifications transfer the risk from you to the contractor. You know exactly what you're paying before work begins.

## Key Cost Components in a Commercial Fit Out Budget

Understanding where your money goes helps you make informed decisions about where to invest and where to economise. Fit out budgets typically break down into three categories.

| Cost Type | What It Covers |
|-----------|----------------|
| Hard costs | Physical construction, M&E, partitions, flooring, joinery |
| Soft costs | Design fees, consultancy, permits, project management |
| Contingency | Risk allowance for unforeseen issues |

### Hard Costs

Hard costs cover the physical work: partitions, suspended ceilings, mechanical and electrical installations, flooring, decoration, and joinery. For most projects, hard costs represent the majority of total spend.

The quality of materials and complexity of installation directly affect this figure. However, experienced contractors can often suggest alternatives that deliver the same result at lower cost — more on that later.

### Soft Costs

Soft costs include design fees, CDM compliance, Building Control submissions, and professional consultancy. For a straightforward office refurbishment, soft costs might represent 10–15% of the total. More complex projects with bespoke design requirements sit higher.

One common mistake: treating soft costs as an afterthought. Design fees, in particular, are often underestimated during early budgeting.

### Contingency and Risk Allowance

Contingency isn't extra money — it's planned money for genuine unknowns. The appropriate amount depends on building age, project complexity, and how developed your design is at the point of pricing.

A detailed survey and fixed-price contract reduce the need for excessive contingency. But some allowance is always sensible, even on well-planned projects.

## Setting a Realistic Fit Out Budget Before Work Begins

The decisions you make before construction starts have the greatest impact on final cost. Investing time in pre-construction planning pays dividends throughout the project.

### Define the Scope and Specification in Detail

Vague briefs lead to vague prices. The more detail you can provide about finishes, furniture requirements, and M&E needs, the more accurate your quotations will be.

You don't need architectural drawings for everything. Even a clear written brief with reference images helps contractors price with confidence. What matters is specificity: "carpet tiles in a mid-grey colourway" is more useful than "flooring throughout."

### Commission a Thorough Site Survey

A professional survey identifies structural issues, M&E capacity, and compliance concerns before they become expensive problems. This is especially important in older buildings or spaces that have been heavily modified over time.

The survey cost is typically a fraction of what you'd spend resolving issues discovered mid-project. Think of it as insurance that also sharpens your pricing.

### Factor in CDM 2015 and Building Regulations Compliance

CDM 2015 (Construction Design and Management Regulations) places legal duties on clients, designers, and contractors for health and safety during construction. Building Regulations govern the technical standards your fit out must meet — fire safety, ventilation, accessibility, and more.

Both carry costs, whether for professional appointments, design modifications, or Building Control fees. Budget for them upfront rather than treating them as afterthoughts.

## How Much Contingency To Include in a Commercial Fit Out Budget

There's no universal percentage that works for every project. The right contingency depends on several factors:

- **Building age and condition:** Older buildings carry more unknowns
- **Complexity of M&E works:** Extensive mechanical and electrical upgrades warrant higher allowance
- **Level of design development:** Less developed designs need greater buffer
- **Quality of site survey:** Thorough surveys reduce risk

A well-surveyed project with a fixed-price contract from an experienced contractor might need only 5–10% contingency. A speculative project in an older building with limited survey information might warrant 15–20%.

## Value Engineering Without Compromising on Quality

Value engineering means optimising cost without reducing functionality or finish quality. It's not about cutting corners — it's about spending wisely on what matters and economising where it doesn't.

### Prioritise Specification Where It Matters Most

Reception areas, meeting rooms, and high-traffic zones benefit from premium finishes. Back-of-house spaces, storage areas, and less visible locations can often accommodate more economical alternatives without affecting the overall impression.

The question isn't "how do we spend less?" but "where does spending more actually make a difference?"

### Choose Cost-Effective Materials and Finishes

Experienced contractors can suggest alternatives that look and perform well at lower cost. A vinyl floor that replicates timber, for instance, might deliver the aesthetic you want at half the price — with better durability in high-traffic areas.

This is where practical on-site knowledge matters. A contractor who has installed hundreds of floor finishes knows which products perform and which don't.

### Standardise Repeatable Elements Across the Space

Consistent door sets, partition systems, and ceiling grids reduce waste and simplify installation. Bespoke elements have their place, but standardisation where possible keeps costs predictable.

Every unique detail requires separate ordering, separate cutting, and separate fitting. Repetition, on the other hand, creates efficiency.

## Managing Fit Out Costs During Delivery

Once construction begins, active cost management keeps the project on track. Planning matters, but so does ongoing attention.

### Track Expenditure Against the Budget Weekly

Regular cost reviews with your contractor catch issues early. A small overspend in week two is manageable; discovering it in week eight is not.

Weekly tracking also creates accountability. When everyone knows the numbers are being watched, decisions tend to be more careful.

### Control Variations With a Formal Change Process

All changes — however small — benefit from written documentation, pricing, and approval before execution. This prevents informal scope creep and ensures everyone understands the cost implications before work proceeds.

The discipline here is simple: nothing changes without a signed variation order. It sounds bureaucratic, but it protects both parties.

### Use Out-of-Hours Working To Reduce Disruption Costs

Weekend or evening work can compress programmes and avoid business downtime. For occupied buildings, this often proves more cost-effective than extended daytime programmes that disrupt operations.

## Choosing the Right Fit Out Contractor for Budget Certainty

The contractor you choose has a significant impact on whether your project stays on budget. Not all contractors operate the same way, and the differences matter.

### In-House Trades Versus Subcontracted Labour

Contractors with employed tradespeople have greater control over quality and programme. Heavy reliance on subcontractors can introduce coordination challenges and inconsistent workmanship.

When the same team works together project after project, communication improves and problems get solved faster.

### Fixed-Price Contracts Versus Open Estimates

Fixed-price contracts provide certainty. Open-book arrangements with provisional sums and daywork rates can inflate final costs significantly.

The question to ask: "Is this price fixed, or is it an estimate?" The answer tells you who carries the risk.

### Proven Track Record of On-Budget Delivery

Ask for references from similar projects. Enquire specifically about how the contractor handled variations and whether projects completed within the original budget.

Past performance isn't a guarantee, but it's the closest thing you'll get to one.
