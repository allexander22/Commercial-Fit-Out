If you're leasing or fitting out a commercial office for the first time, "Cat A" and "Cat B" are two terms you'll hear constantly — and they matter, because they define exactly what condition a space is in when you take it on, and what work still needs to happen before it's usable.

## Cat A fit out: the base build

Category A ("Cat A") fit out is the base standard a landlord typically delivers before handing a floor or unit over to a tenant. It brings the space from a bare shell to a usable, but unbranded and unfurnished, condition. A typical Cat A specification includes:

- Raised access flooring
- Suspended ceilings with a standard lighting grid
- Mechanical and electrical services distributed to the floor (heating, ventilation, power and data first-fix)
- Fire safety systems — detection, alarm and basic compartmentation
- Finished common areas, WCs and often a basic reception
- Blank, decorated walls with no partitioning

In short, Cat A gets a floor ready to occupy in principle, but it's generic — every tenant on every floor of the building typically receives broadly the same specification. It's not built around any one company's way of working.

## Cat B fit out: making it yours

Category B ("Cat B") fit out is where the space becomes specific to the occupying business. This is the layer built on top of Cat A, and it's where a tenant's brand, culture and working patterns actually show up in the finished space. A typical Cat B scope includes:

- Partitioning for meeting rooms, private offices and breakout areas
- Additional ceiling and lighting work tailored to the new layout
- Branded reception and signage
- Kitchen, breakout and collaboration spaces
- IT and AV infrastructure fit-out
- Flooring finishes specific to zones (carpet tile, LVT, etc.)
- Furniture, fixtures and equipment

Cat B is almost always where a business's specific requirements — desk ratios, meeting room count, acoustic separation, accessibility provision — get translated into a built space. It's also the stage most closely tied to a company's actual headcount and working patterns, so it tends to be revisited more often than Cat A as a business grows or its working practices change.

## Why the distinction matters for budgeting

Understanding which category you're inheriting from a landlord — and which you're responsible for delivering yourself — has a direct effect on your budget and programme. Leases vary: some include a Cat A contribution or a "Cat A plus" specification, others hand over genuine shell-and-core space that needs both categories delivered from scratch. Before setting a fit out budget, it's worth clarifying in writing exactly what condition the space will be in on handover, because the answer changes the scope — and cost — of the fit out significantly.
